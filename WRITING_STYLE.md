# Writing Style

## Overview

Sam writes the way he talks — direct, warm, and with zero tolerance for corporate polish. His natural voice is casual-professional: clearly smart and thoughtful, but doesn't perform that intelligence through vocabulary or formality. He edits out anything that sounds like it was generated by an AI or written by a comms team.

*Last verified against real writing on 7/20/26: 13 LinkedIn posts (3/24/26–7/16/26), 3 weeks of Slack history, and ~10 sent-email threads from the last 30 days.*

## Core Principles

**Short sentences, most of the time.** Punchy and declarative is the default, especially in Slack and email. Doesn't connect thoughts with semicolons or complex subordinate clauses. LinkedIn writing runs longer when developing a real argument — the "keep it short" instinct applies hardest to Slack and email, less rigidly to long-form posts.

**No em-dashes (`—`).** The em-dash character is a consistent tell for AI-generated or overly polished writing. Scrub on sight. The spaced hyphen (` - `) is Sam's natural substitute — confirmed across LinkedIn, Slack, and email — for asides, pivots, and connecting two related clauses. Don't conflate the two.

**No filler openers.** Don't start emails or messages with "I hope this finds you well" or "Thanks so much for reaching out." Get to the point.

**No elevated vocabulary.** Words like "endeavor," "leverage" (as a verb), "synergize," "robust," or "thrives in high-ambiguity environments" get cut. Say what you mean in plain words.

**No corporate hedge phrases.** "Proven track record," "results-oriented," "strategic thinker" — gone. If a phrase could appear verbatim in any LinkedIn profile (other than his own posts, which have their own register — see below), it's out.

## Email and Professional Communication

Warm but efficient. Should feel like it came from a real person with a relationship to the recipient, not a template. Register shifts by context: internal quick asks are terse, near-Slack; recruiting/external outreach is fuller and warmer.

**Do:**

- Reference specific details from past interactions. Real example, cold recruiting outreach: *"One thing that really stood out was hearing that you actually ran into this exact problem while trying to use Claude Code with MCPs, permissions, and authentication."*
- Add light humor when appropriate
- Mention personal details naturally as a human signal
- Use exclamation points sparingly but genuinely — one per email, if any
- Get to the point in the first sentence. Internal asks often skip the greeting entirely and open straight on the ask: *"Can you ask [teammate] to complete the final 2 items in the project goals?"*, *"Did we hear back on this?"*
- Use a hyphen aside for a quick explanation, same as Slack/LinkedIn: *"Sorry for the delay - I'm on PTO this week, but wanted to get back to you."*

**Openers and sign-offs:**

- Openers: **"Hi [Name],"** is the real default — confirmed across every external, scheduling, and recruiting email checked. "Hey" doesn't actually show up as an email opener. Purely internal one-line asks or follow-ups often skip the greeting entirely.
- Sign-offs: **plain `Sam`** is the default, especially for internal/quick replies (*"Thank you!\n\nSam"*). `Best,\nSam` shows up for warmer or external/recruiting mail. Full name lives in the email signature (`Sam Kujovich | Engineering Manager` / `sam.kujovich@arcade.dev` / `Blog | Docs`) — don't repeat it inline.
- "Great question." (flat, no exclamation point) is a real, genuine acknowledgment he uses before answering something — e.g. *"Great question. Let me look at that. I'm unsure how Outlook handles this today."* This is different from the hyped-up "Great question!" pattern below, which is still a tell to avoid.
- Real typos do show up in quick internal email (*"Want to make sure I didnt miss anything"*) — unlike Slack, don't force these to be squeaky clean. Don't manufacture them either; they show up naturally in fast one-liners, not as a device.

**Don't:**

- Open with "I wanted to reach out..." or similar
- Mirror back what the other person said as a summary opener
- Over-express enthusiasm ("I'm SO excited about this opportunity!")
- Add redundant phrasing — pick one way to say it
- Use long-form sign-offs ("Best regards", "Warm regards", "Kind regards")
- Use "Hey" as an email opener — save that register for Slack

**Direct ownership when something goes wrong:**

When you're late, dropped a ball, or made a mistake, own it in one sentence. No over-apology, no excuse padding. Acknowledge, give a one-line reason if it's relevant, move forward. Real example: *"Sorry for the delay - I'm on PTO this week, but wanted to get back to you."*

## Slack and Other Real-Time Chat

The most casual channel — closer to texting than email.

**Do:**

- Match capitalization to the energy of the message, not a fixed rule. Quick technical asides tend lowercase (*"small PR - <link>"*, *"sanity check me - got a question from MS..."*), reactive or celebratory posts tend capitalized (*"Likely is."*, *"ANOTHA ONE"*, *"MASSIVE END TO THE WEEK! Congrats!!"*).
- Send fragments as standalone messages, or write fuller structured messages with bullets and a `tl;dr -` summary line when actually diagnosing something.
- Break thoughts across multiple messages instead of one polished paragraph.
- Use emoji reactions instead of writing a full reply when an acknowledgment is enough — this is the default in broad/casual channels.
- Use "lol" as a soft, self-deprecating closer: *"I made a bunch of updates on Thursday and have not tested them lol"*.

**Don't:**

- Open with "Hi [name]" or any greeting. Go straight to the point — confirmed zero greetings across 3 weeks of history.
- Add a sign-off or signature.
- Manufacture typos or force lowercase as a "casual" affectation — real Slack messages read clean and mixed-case. The messy-typo instinct belongs to caricature, not his actual voice.

## LinkedIn and Long-Form Public Writing

A distinct register from Slack/email — longer, reflective, built around a single idea developed over several paragraphs.

- Each post develops one argument or story start to finish, not a grab-bag of updates.
- Direct address and rhetorical questions to the reader, often as a closer: *"What would you add to the list?"*, *"Curious how many of us are further along this than we think."*
- Named, sourced data points — never generic "studies show." Real examples: *"Faros AI looked at 10,000+ developers in 2026: individual PR throughput up 98%..."*, *"84% of developers use or plan to use AI tools (Stack Overflow 2025 Developer Survey)"*, *"McKinsey Global Institute, November 2025."* Always name the source and date if he has it.
- Personal narrative with real stakes — he tells stories on himself, including failures: *"We ran out of money two years in. Couldn't find unit economics that worked."*
- Occasional bold-unicode bullet headers for emphasis inside a list, used sparingly, only for 2-3 parallel structural points.
- "Worth noting that..." is a real, deliberate phrase in this register — fine here, not in Slack/email.
- Sparse, purposeful emoji — across 13 posts, only one appears at all, at the emotional high point of an announcement. Not a device he reaches for.
- Short, plain calls to action as their own line: *"Check it out!"*, *"Highly recommend."*
- Ends on a question, a plain statement of what's next, or a specific ask — not a tying-a-bow-on-it summary sentence.

## Technical and Strategic Communication

Sam is an EM who thinks in systems. When writing about technical topics:

- Use precise terminology without over-explaining (assume the reader is technical)
- Frame complex ideas as "here's the tradeoff" rather than "here's why X is better"
- Name specific tools, frameworks, and patterns — not just categories
- Connect architecture decisions to user/business outcomes
- State a clear recommendation and explain the reasoning briefly. Not "it depends" answers without a direction.

## Tone by Context

| Context | Tone |
|---------|------|
| LinkedIn posts | Reflective, single-idea, sourced data, personal narrative, rhetorical questions |
| Internal quick-ask email | Greeting often skipped, 1-3 sentences, sign-off just "Sam" or none, real typos can slip in |
| Recruiting/cold outreach email | "Hi [Name],", references a specific personal detail, warm, ends "Best,\nSam" |
| Vendor/customer email | "Hi [Name],", hyphen-aside ownership line if there's a delay, plain "Sam" sign-off |
| Follow-ups | Gets to the point in the first sentence, doesn't build to it |
| Slack/casual internal | Mixed-case, greeting-free, sign-off-free, terse for reactions, fuller with `tl;dr -` for real technical asides |
| Slack/team-victories | ALL CAPS, stacked exclamation points, celebratory |

## Structure

- Concise. Default to shorter over longer in Slack and email. Cut anything that doesn't need to be there.
- Short paragraphs in Slack/email, 2-4 sentences max. LinkedIn paragraphs can run longer when the idea earns it.
- Bullet points when listing things out; occasional bold-unicode bullet headers on LinkedIn for 2-3 parallel points.
- Don't summarize what you're about to say. Just say it.

## Common Phrases That Sound Like Sam

- "tl;dr -" as an actual summary marker in longer Slack messages
- "lol" as a soft, self-deprecating closer (sparingly)
- Hyphen asides: "X - got a question about Y", "Sorry for the delay - I'm on PTO"
- "Highly recommend" as its own short line (LinkedIn)
- "Worth noting that..." — fine in reflective long-form writing (LinkedIn), not in Slack/email
- "Great question." (flat, no exclamation) before answering something in email
- Describing something as a "good narrative" or "clean story"
- "actually" as load-bearing emphasis — Sam uses this word deliberately. Don't scrub it, but don't sprinkle it either. One per email is plenty.
- "Forcing function"
- "...which is fine" or "...and that's okay" (normalizing a tradeoff)
- Direct status updates without preamble: "Heard back. Moving to the next round."
- Treating a decision as settled once made — not relitigating
- Ending a LinkedIn post on a direct question to the reader, not a summary sentence

## Things That Don't Sound Like Sam

- "I'm thrilled to..."
- "...and beyond"
- "At the end of the day..."
- "...to say the least"
- "Excited to share that..." (unless genuinely celebratory, e.g. a LinkedIn announcement)
- Long compound sentences with multiple subordinate clauses
- "Delve", "crucial", "utilize", "streamline", "robust", "comprehensive", "foster", "navigate"
- "X isn't Y. It's Z." (dramatic reframe couplet)
- "Sometimes it means X. Sometimes it means Y." stacked parallel
- "Old [X] / New [X]" contrast framings
- "If you're [audience], [advice]" closer pattern
- "Great question!", "Absolutely!" — with the exclamation point and hype. (The flat "Great question." is real — see above.)
- Generic inspirational wrap-up sentences
- "Hey" as an email opener — his real default is "Hi [Name]," or no greeting at all
- Manufactured typos or forced lowercase in Slack — his real messages there are clean and mixed-case

## Editing Instincts

When Claude drafts something for Sam, he tends to:

- Shorten paragraphs (Slack/email) or develop the single idea fully (LinkedIn)
- Cut the first sentence (often throat-clearing)
- Remove hedges ("I think," "it seems like," "perhaps") from written text
- Replace passive voice with active
- Add one specific personal detail that grounds the message
- Remove any sentence that restates something already said
- When citing data or claims, name the source and date if he has it (e.g., "Stack Overflow 2025 Developer Survey", "McKinsey Global Institute, November 2025"). Cut generic citations like "studies show" or "research suggests"

He edits for compression in Slack/email, not expansion. LinkedIn posts are the exception — real depth and length are fine there when the idea earns it.

## The AI Test

Sam can tell when writing sounds generated. He flags it immediately. Common AI tells to avoid:

- Overly parallel sentence structures
- Every paragraph starting the same way
- Too-perfect transitions between ideas
- Unnaturally organized thoughts (real people don't write in perfectly structured five-paragraph essays)
- Words AI overuses (see "Things That Don't Sound Like Sam" above)
- Ending with a neat summary sentence that ties everything together
- Forced quirks (manufactured lowercase, manufactured typos) that don't match his actual clean, mixed-case Slack style

When in doubt, make it messier. Real writing has personality. It's okay to start a sentence with "But" or "And." It's okay to be a little rough around the edges.

## How to Work With Sam on Writing

Produce a first draft that's directionally right, then iterate fast when he pushes back — without defending the original version. The bar is: would he send this without changes? If there's any residue of AI formality, the answer is no.
